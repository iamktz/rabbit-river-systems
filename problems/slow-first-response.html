Slow first response

They contacted you Tuesday. You got back Thursday. They'd already hired someone.

What's actually happening

Inquiries don't arrive when you're at a desk. They arrive when you're on a roof, under a sink, or driving between jobs with both hands busy. So they pile up, and you work through them at night when you're tired and the day has already taken everything.

Meanwhile the person who contacted you didn't contact only you. They contacted three companies in about six minutes, because that's what the internet made easy. From their side, this isn't a decision yet. It's a race, and they're not even watching it.

What it costs you

The first real response wins far more often than it should — not because that company is better, but because it was there. By the time you call back, the job isn't harder to win. It's already gone, and nobody tells you.

That's what makes this one expensive. You never see it happen. It shows up months later as a quieter phone and a vague sense that the market got tougher.

What it looks like

Somebody fills out your form at 9:40 on a Tuesday morning. You're on a job. You see it at 6:15 that evening, decide it's too late to call, and put it on tomorrow's list. You call Wednesday at 8:30 and get voicemail.

They booked someone Tuesday at 11:00. You were never in the running, and you spent a day and a half thinking you were.

Where I'd start

The fix isn't answering faster. You can't — you have a job to do. The fix is making sure something answers, so the clock stops running against you.

  • An automatic reply that confirms you got it and says when you'll call. This alone stops most of the bleeding.
  • A number that texts back rather than dumping people into voicemail.
  • One window a day, same time, where inquiries get handled before they age.

None of that requires new software you have to learn. It requires deciding that the first hour matters more than it feels like it does.

Not sure if this is the one costing you?

That's the usual situation, and it's worth twenty minutes. Most owners guess wrong about which of the seven is theirs — the loudest problem is almost never the expensive one.
