← Back to Tech & AIDo I Have Too Many Tools?

Keep What Works. Remove What Doesn’t.

Look at what your tools do, what they cost, and where they create work. The goal is less friction, not a smaller app count.

The problem

Count the friction, not just the tools.

Several useful applications can work well together. A handful can still leave people retyping information, searching multiple places, or working around unclear ownership. Review value before deciding what to remove.

Purpose, people, and use
What job does each tool do? Who depends on it, who administers it, and is it used? Check overlapping features before assuming duplication is waste.
Information and handoffs
Where does important information live? Who copies, downloads, uploads, or reconciles it? Decide which record is authoritative.
Dependencies and true cost
What breaks if a tool disappears? Include subscriptions, training, support, access, integrations, switching effort, and records that must be kept.
Tool Stack Reality Check · Available by request

Keep what works. Remove what doesn’t. Connect what should work together.

Start with one team or workflow and an agreed set of tools. Bring the tool list, renewal costs, who uses them, and examples of repeated friction.

  1. Map what is there

    Record purpose, owner, actual use, information, cost, and important dependencies.

  2. Find the avoidable work

    Identify overlap, unused capacity, double entry, confusing records, and features you already pay for.

  3. Make the decisions

    Take away keep, remove, consolidate, connect, or reconsider recommendations with the reasons and uncertainties.

  4. Plan a safe transition

    Before retiring anything, name the owner, preserve needed records, check dependencies, and test the replacement path.

This is an agreed review, not a self-service inventory tool or a commitment to replace your software.

A subscription saving can create an operating cost.

Illustrative example

Two tools both store customer information.

One may schedule work while the other holds service history. Removing either without understanding those roles can create more work. Agree where each fact belongs, then assess whether a supported connection would help.
