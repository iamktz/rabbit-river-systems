AI agents can run
my business. Right?
Right?

Up to a point. And the point has a number on it.

The question hides an assumption

The assumption is that "running the business" is one thing, so the answer has to be yes or no. It isn't one thing. It's a few hundred separate tasks, and every one of them sits somewhere on a ladder of authority.

Once you can see the ladder, the argument mostly stops. You don't have to decide whether you trust AI. You have to decide, per task, how much authority the job actually requires.

The ladder

  • 1 · Read. Can look, cannot change. Summarize a document, search your own files.
  • 2 · Draft. Prepares something, a person decides whether to use it. A proposal, a reply, a first pass.
  • 3 · Write. Changes information inside your business. Updates a record, edits a file, creates a task.
  • 4 · Send or publish. Reaches the outside world. Emails a customer. Posts something with your name on it.
  • 5 · Delete or spend. Hard to reverse. Deletes records, runs code, buys things.
  • 6 · Authorize. Hands authority to something else. Creates logins, approves payments, starts other automations.

Levels one through three: yes. Genuinely, usefully, and you are leaving money on the table if you aren't. Four through six unattended: no. Not because the technology can't, but because the consequences don't reverse and nothing in the output tells you which ones went wrong.

So the real answer to the question is: an agent can run a great deal of the work and none of the accountability, and almost everybody selling you "AI transformation" is quietly hoping you won't notice which side of four they're describing.

Ask the people furthest ahead

Eric Siu runs a marketing agency built on this promise. He has every commercial reason to tell you the machines have it handled, and he put out a video walking through his entire setup, screen by screen.

He called it "I Check Its Work."

He wasn't being modest. The agent drafts his carousels and he sends them back. It writes an exit popup and he says on camera that it isn't good enough. It builds a landing page and he calls it a start. His own number: it gets him sixty or seventy percent of the way, he takes it to eighty-five, and a person on his team takes it from there.

Watch where he actually sits on the ladder. Levels one through three, running hot, all day. And a human being standing at four.

Why four is the line

Bad output that looks bad is harmless. You see it, you delete it, you lose four minutes.

Bad output that looks good is the whole problem. It's formatted correctly. It's confident. It has the right shape. It quotes a warranty term that isn't in your warranty, or reassures a customer about a lead time you can't hit.

Nothing about the output signals which kind it is. The plausibility is uniform. That isn't a bug someone patches next year, it's what the thing fundamentally is, and the only instrument that reads the difference is a person who already knows the answer.

At levels one through three, that person gets a chance to look. At four and above, the customer sees it first.

What transformation actually means

Not handing over the business. Two things, both unglamorous.

Move as much work as you can down to levels one through three, where a wrong answer is cheap and someone still looks at it. That's where the genuine gains are, and they are large. Then be deliberate about who approves at four and above, on purpose, written down, rather than by whoever configured the integration on a Tuesday.

The constraint people miss is not the model. It's qualified attention. If nobody in your business can tell a good answer from a plausible one in a given area, you do not have an AI opportunity there. You have a knowledge gap, and the AI will widen it at speed.

Where I'd start

Not with a strategy. With an inventory, because most businesses have already granted more authority than they remember granting. Somebody clicked "Sign in with Google" eighteen months ago and nobody has looked since.

The version I'll defend

AI can run processes. It cannot run a business, because a business is the accumulated set of decisions about which processes deserve to exist and who answers for them. Judgment, accountability, taste. None of the three have shown any sign of arriving in a model release.

I use these tools hard, every day, for research and analysis and building. I've also watched them produce a beautifully formatted answer that was entirely wrong, which is why anything I hand you gets reviewed by me first, and why I'll separate what was observed from what still needs checking.

The people getting real value here are not the ones who handed over the work. They're the ones who got much faster at producing it and stayed exactly as responsible for whether it was any good.

No fluff. No hype. No science fiction. That last one especially, because it's the trick you're most likely to fall for: most of what gets sold as AI transformation is a real capability described about a year early, in the present tense, to somebody who has no way to check.
