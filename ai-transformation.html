AI agents will run my
entire business, right?
Right?

There is useful work to hand over. Deciding which work—and on what terms—is still your job.

Start with the job, not the agent.

A missed inquiry, a slow quote, information copied between systems: these are business problems before they are technology decisions. Sometimes the answer is AI. Sometimes it is a better form, a reliable reminder, or a process somebody can actually follow.

My starting question is not “Where can we put AI?” It is “What keeps going wrong, and what would a useful improvement look like?” Technology should earn its place in the answer.

Your business is not one task.

Preparing a reply, checking a price, sending a promise, and taking a payment have different consequences. Treating them as one automation hides the decisions that matter.

Break the work into steps. Identify the information each step needs, who owns the result, and what happens when it fails. Then decide what to delegate, what to review, and what should stay with a person.

Illustrative example · not a customer result

An inquiry arrives after hours.

Before buying an agent, find out what happens today. Are inquiries being missed, or simply waiting until morning? What information is needed? Who follows up?

A first test might capture the request, draft a summary, and put it in front of the person responsible. Compare response time, missed requests, and correction effort with the current process. Decide whether an automated customer-facing reply is justified only after testing its accuracy, boundaries, and failure handling.

A small improvement you can inspect is worth more than a demonstration that quietly assumes everything works.

Access is a business decision.

Reading a file, changing a record, contacting a customer, spending money, and granting another system access are different permissions. Even read-only access can expose sensitive information. Internal changes can also cause harm.

Give a tool only the access the task needs. Define approval points, keep a record of actions, and know how to stop or reverse the process where possible. The right controls depend on the task and its consequences—not a blanket claim that one category of AI work is safe.

There is more to technology than AI.

A clearer handoff, a well-chosen existing tool, or removing duplicate work may solve the problem without a new model or agent. For familiar problems, look at proven approaches first. Experiment where the work or the available tools have genuinely changed.

That is the point of view I bring: understand the constraint, test a bounded improvement, and measure whether it helped. No need to give an ordinary workflow a science-fiction job title.
