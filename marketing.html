Decide who you serve, what you offer, and why it matters. Then help the right people find it.

The old joke still earns its keep.

What’s the difference between sales, engineering, and marketing?

Sales can’t tell the truth. Engineering can’t tell a lie. And marketing can’t tell the difference.

I’ve used that joke for years. As a former salesperson and systems engineer, I have managed to stand on more than one side of the accusation.

It is a joke, of course. The useful tension behind it is real: a business needs to understand what it can deliver, explain why it matters, and earn the customer’s confidence. Marketing helps connect those decisions.

Start before the posts and the ads.

A website, a campaign, and a polished brochure can all do useful work. They work better when the business has settled a few questions first:

  • Who is this for, and what are they trying to accomplish?
  • What can they buy from you, at what price, and with what boundaries?
  • Why would they choose it, and what evidence supports the promise?
  • Where will they find you, and what should happen next?

My bias is to make those decisions visible before spending more on promotion. A beautifully formatted uncertainty is still an uncertainty. It just has better margins.

Illustrative example

One contractor. Three different customers.

Imagine a contractor offering emergency repairs, premium remodels, and commercial maintenance. Each can be a legitimate part of the business. Each buyer arrives with a different question.

The emergency customer wants availability. The remodel customer wants evidence of workmanship and an explanation of the process. The facilities manager wants to understand service coverage and reliability.

A clearer website gives those people distinct paths. More promotion might bring more visitors; the paths help those visitors recognize the right service and take a useful next step.

A test you can run today.

Describe who an offer is for and what it helps them accomplish in one sentence. Ask someone outside the business to explain it back.

If their version surprises you, investigate the gap. You may need a clearer sentence, a more specific offer, or better evidence. That is useful work to do before ordering another batch of brochures.

A dashboard should help you decide.

Reach, engagement, and clicks can tell you whether people are noticing. Qualified inquiries, customer conversations, purchases, and repeat business can help you understand what follows.

Those measures answer different questions. Attribution can be imperfect, especially when a customer encounters a business several times before making contact. Be explicit about what the evidence supports and what is still uncertain.

My favorite question for a report is: “What decision would we change because of this?” If the answer is unclear, the dashboard may need a smaller wardrobe.
