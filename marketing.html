Marketing

Will they understand you
before they choose you?

Customers need to know whether you can help. So do the AI systems they may use to research the decision.

The old joke still earns its keep.

What’s the difference between sales, engineering, and marketing?

Sales can’t tell the truth. Engineering can’t tell a lie. And marketing can’t tell the difference.

I’ve used that joke for years. As a former salesperson and systems engineer, I have managed to stand on more than one side of the accusation.

The joke depends on our thinking we know what Sales and Engineering do. Marketing is the one we’re less sure about. Posts? Ads? The logo? That uncertainty is the useful punchline—not the idea that marketers can’t tell the truth.

Marketing helps a business understand whom it serves, shape an offer worth choosing, explain why it matters, and make it possible to find and buy. That is critical work. Promotion is one part of it.

Start before the posts and the ads.

A website, a campaign, and a polished brochure can all do useful work. They work better when the business has settled a few questions first:

  • Who is this for, and what are they trying to accomplish?
  • What can they buy from you, at what price, and with what boundaries?
  • Why would they choose it, and what evidence supports the promise?
  • Where will they find you, and what should happen next?

My bias is to make those decisions visible before spending more on promotion. A beautifully formatted uncertainty is still an uncertainty. It just has better margins.

Illustrative example

One contractor. Three different customers.

Imagine a contractor offering emergency repairs, premium remodels, and commercial maintenance. Each can be a legitimate part of the business. Each buyer arrives with a different question.

The emergency customer wants availability. The remodel customer wants evidence of workmanship and an explanation of the process. The facilities manager wants to understand service coverage and reliability.

A clearer website gives those people distinct paths. More promotion might bring more visitors; the paths help those visitors recognize the right service and take a useful next step.

Now there is another reader to consider.

A customer may ask an AI system to compare providers before visiting a website. Can your public information answer the questions that matter: what you do, where you work, who you help, what the limits are, and how to take the next step?

Being understood comes before being acted on.

Agent-to-agent interaction raises a further question: if a customer delegates part of a task, what can a system reliably learn or do with your business? Discovering an offer and being authorized to book, buy, or make a commitment are different things.

My view: start with clear, accurate business information and an understandable path to contact. Consider an agent interface only when a real use case warrants it. A2A is a direction to evaluate, not a reason to replace your website or install infrastructure before you need it.

What do customers ask that your website never answers?

Start with a few real questions from inquiries or sales conversations. Compare them with the public answers. Look for missing information, contradictions, outdated claims, and vague promises. Keep private customer details private; publish the useful answer, not somebody’s personal record.

That comparison can reveal a practical next step without another campaign or a mountain of generated content. Clearer answers help a person decide whether to contact you. They also give systems better source material to work with—without guaranteeing what those systems will say.
