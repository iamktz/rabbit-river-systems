Nobody knows what
marketing is.

I've opened with the same joke for twenty years and it still lands, which ought to worry the people it's about.

The joke

What's the difference between sales, engineering, and marketing?

Sales can't tell the truth. Engineering can't tell a lie. And marketing can't tell the difference.

Every room laughs, and the engineers laugh loudest, which tells you something about engineers. But the joke keeps working for a reason that isn't flattering to anybody in it, and the reason is the point of this page.

Why it lands

Sales lives with a customer who is deciding, so it leans toward whatever makes the decision happen. Engineering lives with physics, which does not negotiate, so it says the true thing even when the true thing loses the deal.

Marketing sits between them, and its actual job is translation: take the true thing and make it land on a person who has eleven seconds and no reason to care. That is genuinely hard, and from either side it looks like fudging.

So the joke is a libel, but a fair one, because marketing done badly really is indistinguishable from not knowing the difference. And marketing is done badly most of the time, for a reason nobody finds funny.

The confusion that costs money

Almost everybody uses the word "marketing" to mean the visible output. The logo. The posts. The ads. The website. The brochure nobody reads.

That's promotion. Promotion is one chore inside marketing, and it's the last one.

Marketing is the set of decisions that comes before it. Who is this for, specifically enough that you could point at one. What are you actually offering them, in their words and not yours. What does it cost and why is that the right number. Where will they run into it. What has to be true for them to believe you.

Make those decisions and the promotion mostly writes itself, because there is finally something to say. Skip them and no amount of promotion fixes it. You end up paying somebody to say nothing more professionally.

What this looks like in a real business

A contractor decides he needs marketing. Somebody sells him a package: twelve social posts a month, a refreshed website, a bit of ad spend. Fair price, competent work, everybody honest.

Six months later there's a nicer website and no more calls. So he concludes marketing doesn't work for his trade, which is now a belief he'll carry for a decade.

Nobody ever asked him who he was for. He does high-end remodels and emergency repairs and light commercial, and his site speaks to all three, which means it speaks to nobody. Every visitor has to do the sorting themselves, and visitors don't sort. They leave.

That is not a promotion failure. The promotion did its job. It carried an undecided message faster and farther.

A test you can run today

Say who you're for and what you do for them, in one sentence, out loud.

Now imagine a stranger repeating it to a friend a week later. Would they get it roughly right?

If the answer is no, more marketing will not help you, and more promotion will hurt, because promotion is a multiplier and you are multiplying something blurry. Fix the sentence first. The sentence is cheap. The ad spend is not.

The most hand-waved data-driven job there is

No function in a business claims data more loudly than marketing. Impressions, reach, engagement, click-through, attribution, a dashboard with nine panels on it. The vocabulary is relentlessly quantitative.

And no function is vaguer about whether any of it produced money.

Every other part of a company gets graded on a number that bites. Sales has revenue, and it is not open to interpretation: you closed it or you did not. Operations has cost and throughput. Finance has the actual books, and the books do not care how the quarter felt. Marketing has a dashboard where everything moves, everything is up and to the right if you pick the right window, and nobody will stake their own money on any single line of it.

The unfair version of this is that marketers are dodging. Mostly they are not. The honest version is harder: the thing marketing actually changes is whether a stranger believes you, and that is genuinely difficult to measure. Meanwhile the things that are easy to measure are largely the things that do not matter much. So you get a discipline that measures what it can and then reports it as though it had measured what it should. Everyone in the room knows the difference and the slide goes up anyway.

Which gives you a test you can run in any meeting. When someone shows you marketing results, ask which of those numbers they would bet their own money on. Not a trick question, and not hostile. It just separates the numbers that track attention from the numbers that track money, and watching which way the conversation goes tells you most of what you need to know about who you are dealing with.

Where I come into this
